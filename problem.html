The policy problem

Trial technology is a target, and binding device rules stop at classification

The policy problem. A wearable or app used in a decentralized clinical trial can fall outside every binding device cybersecurity rule in the United States and the European Union. The systems that hold its data face binding rules, such as Part 11 and GDPR Article 32, that name no security standard. And the regulators who inspect trials have no shared way to compare what they find.

This page makes the case in four steps: the data is already being taken, trial technology is growing fast, the rules depend on a label, and each group in a trial carries a different cost.

1. Health data from clinical research is already being taken

Health care data breaches are frequent and often run through vendors. As of a July 7, 2025 snapshot of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services breach portal, reported by BankInfoSecurity, 345 breaches had been reported in 2025. They affected about 29.9 million people. Of those, 127 breaches, 37 percent, involved a business associate, and they affected about 15.8 million people, more than half of the total.

Consumer wearable platforms have been hit. In July 2020 a ransomware attack took Garmin's Connect service offline for several days. In 2018, Bellingcat and De Correspondent showed that Polar's Flow app exposed the homes and exercise routes of military and intelligence personnel. Neither involved a trial, but Garmin is one of the five brands that Miyakoshi and Ito counted in trial registrations.

What this does not show. None of these incidents involved a trial wearable, and I found no documented breach of one, so my argument rests on exposure. This data is a target, and decentralized trials add more devices and more vendors. I argue for acting before a breach occurs.

Data theft is one risk. Altered data is another. If a sensor's readings or a platform's records can be changed without detection, a trial's results can be wrong, and regulators base approval decisions on those results. Part 11 requires controls that protect the integrity of electronic records (§11.10), and ICH E6(R3) is written to produce reliable trial results. None of the incidents above is known to have changed trial data; the risk is my reading.

2. Trial technology is growing, and for good reasons

FDA describes why trials are moving toward participants' homes. Decentralized trials “have the potential to expand access to more representative patient populations and improve trial efficiencies.” By enabling remote participation they may reduce the burden on caregivers and “facilitate research on rare diseases and diseases affecting populations with limited mobility or limited access to traditional clinical trial sites.”

The same guidance says digital health technologies “have expanded the types of trial-related data that can be obtained remotely.” That expansion also puts a sensor on a wrist inside a trial's data path.

3. The rules depend on a label

In both systems, binding device cybersecurity rules attach to products that are classified as medical devices. The U.S. rule, §524B, applies when a maker submits a connected device to FDA. The EU rule, MDR Annex I, applies to products intended for a medical purpose. A wrist sensor sold for fitness is not a device because a trial uses it, so neither rule reaches it. The EU's Cyber Resilience Act might, but its text never mentions clinical trials and its reach to a tool supplied only inside a trial is unsettled. In the U.S., the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule may reach some health apps that HIPAA does not, but it requires notice after a breach and sets no security requirements.

Policy gap 1: the sensor

No binding U.S. rule makes the maker of an unsubmitted trial wearable find, disclose or patch its flaws.

Policy gap 2: the systems

None of the instruments reviewed names a technical security standard for eConsent and eCOA platforms. Standards such as IEC 81001-5-1 and ISO/IEC 27001 exist, but no U.S. or EU trial rule or guidance points to one.

Policy gap 3: the inspections

FDA and its partners share inspection findings, and the program as described covers data integrity and does not mention cybersecurity.

Why the policy gap persists

Binding rules aimed at connected products arrived after decentralized trials took off: §524B applies from March 2023 and the Cyber Resilience Act's main duties from December 2027. The EU's health data space, which is a data rule, begins in 2027. FDA's decentralized-trial guidance and its Q&A date from 2024 and are nonbinding.

A trial that starts today could run into 2028 without knowing which EU rules apply to its wearable. And data collected now can be reused for research in the EU from 2029, with clinical trial data as a category from 2031, so weak security at the sensor can affect the data when it is reused.

Terms in plain language

For readers new to clinical trials.

Decentralized clinical trial (DCT). FDA's definition: “A clinical trial that includes decentralized elements where trial-related activities occur at locations other than traditional clinical trial sites.” Parts of the study happen at a participant's home.
Digital health technology (DHT). FDA's definition: a system that uses computing platforms, connectivity, software or sensors for health care and related uses, spanning “applications in general wellness to applications as a medical device.” A trial wearable is one.
Wearable. A sensor worn on the body, such as a wrist band that records heart rate and sleep.
eConsent. The informed-consent process done on a screen instead of on paper.
eCOA. Electronic clinical outcome assessment: the symptom surveys, diaries and tests that participants complete on a phone or tablet.
Sponsor. The organization that runs and pays for a trial, often a drug or device company or a university.
Classified as a medical device. A product that regulators treat as a medical device because of what its maker says it is for. In both the U.S. and the EU, classification decides which device cybersecurity rules apply.
Soft law. Guidance that regulators publish and expect people to follow but that no one is legally required to obey.
IMDRF. The International Medical Device Regulators Forum, a voluntary group of medical device regulators from around the world.
GCP inspection. A regulator's check that a trial follows good clinical practice: that it protects participants and produces reliable data.
Software bill of materials. A list of the software components inside a product, similar to an ingredients list for code.
