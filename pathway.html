Illustrative scenario: one trial, one wrist sensor tracking heart rate and sleep, participants in North Carolina and Germany. It is not a real trial.

U.S.
Participant in North Carolina
  • §524B binds the sensor’s maker only if it submits the sensor to FDA as a cyber device. A consumer-grade wearable that is never submitted is outside the rule.
  • FDA’s guidance and Q&A recommend a risk assessment and written vendor agreements. They apply to participant-owned devices as recommendations.
  • The FTC’s Health Breach Notification Rule requires notice after a breach by health apps outside HIPAA. Whether a sponsor-supplied trial tool counts is unsettled, and the rule sets no security requirements.
EU
Participant in Germany
  • MDR Annex I binds the sensor only if its maker intends it for a medical purpose.
  • The Cyber Resilience Act may reach a consumer-type wearable made available on the market. Whether it reaches one supplied only inside a trial is unsettled.

Governing instruments at this step

  • FD&C Act §524B
  • FDA guidance on DCT technology
  • FTC Health Breach Notification Rule
  • MDR Annex I
  • Cyber Resilience Act
  • IMDRF N60, N70, N73

Policy gap at this step

No settled rule on either side makes the sensor’s maker run a vulnerability process unless the product is classified.

Policy Recommendation 1
U.S.
Participant in North Carolina
  • Part 11 sets controls on records that FDA requires, such as access limits and audit trails. It names no technical standard.
  • HIPAA often does not apply to data a sponsor collects directly from participants.
  • FDA’s Q&A recommends a risk assessment and a written agreement with each IT provider that covers data security.
EU
Participant in Germany
  • GDPR Article 32 requires security appropriate to the risk, and Article 33 requires breach notice within 72 hours where feasible.
  • ICH E6(R3) §4.3.3 lists security controls to consider: authentication, firewalls, patching, monitoring, penetration testing.

Governing instruments at this step

  • 21 CFR Part 11
  • HIPAA Security Rule
  • FDA guidance on DCT technology
  • GDPR (Arts. 32–33, Ch. V)
  • ICH E6(R3)

Policy gap at this step

General rules apply to the systems. None names a standard a platform vendor should meet.

Policy response

Policy Recommendation 1
U.S.
Participant in North Carolina
  • Neither the HIPAA Security Rule nor Part 11 is a data-transfer rule.
  • FDA’s drug center shares GCP inspection information with EMA, PMDA, MHRA and Health Canada under confidentiality arrangements. Its program page cites data integrity and does not mention cybersecurity.
EU
Participant in Germany
  • GDPR Chapter V sets the conditions for sending personal data out of the EU: an adequacy decision, safeguards, or a derogation.
  • The Commission’s adequacy page, checked November 22, 2026, lists the United States for commercial organisations in the EU-US Data Privacy Framework.

Governing instruments at this step

  • GDPR (Arts. 32–33, Ch. V)
  • ICH E6(R3)

Policy gap at this step

GDPR and the EHDS cover moving the data. On the inspection side, two regulators can look at the same trial and have no common way to say what a cybersecurity finding is.

Policy response

Policy Recommendation 2
U.S.
Participant in North Carolina
  • No U.S. counterpart in the set of instruments reviewed.
EU
Participant in Germany
  • From March 2029, research reuse goes only through a secure processing environment that must meet listed security measures (Art. 73).
  • Data from wellness applications and from medical devices become reusable categories from 2029. Clinical trial data becomes a category from March 2031 (Arts. 51 and 105).
  • Transfers of personal data to third countries follow GDPR Chapter V (Art. 90).

Governing instruments at this step

  • European Health Data Space
  • GDPR (Arts. 32–33, Ch. V)

Policy gap at this step

The secure environment protects the pool of data. Nothing in it governs the sensor upstream. Recommendation 1 addresses the sensor.
