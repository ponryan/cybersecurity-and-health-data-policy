The policy problem

A wearable or app used in a decentralized trial can fall outside every binding device cybersecurity rule on both sides of the Atlantic. The systems that hold its data face binding rules that name no security standard, and the regulators who inspect trials have no shared way to compare what they find.

Two policy recommendations

Extend IMDRF's device cybersecurity guidance to trial technology, and add cybersecurity findings to the GCP inspection information FDA already shares with EU and other regulators. Read the policy recommendations

Selected on the left
FD&C Act §524B
Binding law. Effective Mar 2023.
In plain language

If a medical device runs software, can connect to the internet and has features that could be vulnerable to attack, its maker has to include cybersecurity information when it asks FDA to clear or approve the device. It applies to devices that go through FDA's premarket review.

Where it aligns
MDR Annex I. Both sides bind classified devices.
Where it diverges
Cyber Resilience Act. Its reach to unclassified trial wearables is unsettled; §524B has no such reach.
Source: Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act §524B, added by the Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act of 2022, §3305 (enacted December 29, 2022).

FDA guidance on DCT technology
Guidance. Not binding.
In plain language

Two FDA guidances tell sponsors how to handle technology in decentralized trials. One covers decentralized trials as a whole. The other answers questions about electronic systems, records and signatures. They set out what FDA recommends. They do not create legal duties.

Where it aligns
IMDRF guidance. Both are soft law.
Where it diverges
Cyber Resilience Act. The EU instrument binds if it reaches the product, and this guidance is nonbinding.
Source: FDA, Conducting Clinical Trials With Decentralized Elements: Guidance for Industry, Investigators, and Other Interested Parties (September 2024), sections on digital health technologies and electronic systems.
Open the full statute page
21 CFR Part 11
Binding rule. In force.
In plain language

Part 11 sets the conditions under which FDA treats electronic records and electronic signatures as trustworthy and equivalent to paper. It covers records that FDA's rules require and records submitted to FDA.

Where it aligns
ICH E6(R3). Both expect trustworthy electronic data.
Where it diverges
GDPR. It covers any personal data, including data outside FDA-required records.
Source: 21 CFR Part 11, Electronic Records; Electronic Signatures (eCFR, current as of October 13, 2026), §§11.1, 11.10 and 11.30.
Open the full statute page
HIPAA Security Rule
Binding rule. In force.
In plain language

The Security Rule requires safeguards for electronic protected health information held by covered entities and their business associates. It tells each organization what its security program must achieve and leaves the choice of tools to the organization.

Where it aligns
GDPR Article 32. Both require appropriate security for personal data.
Where it diverges
GDPR. It reaches any data controller, which is wider than HIPAA's covered entities and business associates.
Source: 45 CFR §160.103 (definitions) and 45 CFR Part 164, Subpart C (Title 45, 10-1-25 edition).
Open the full statute page
FTC Health Breach Notification Rule
Binding rule. Amended 2024.
In plain language

Health apps and similar technologies that HIPAA does not cover must notify users, the FTC and sometimes the media within 60 days of a breach of identifiable health information. The FTC amended the rule in 2024 to make clear that it covers health apps.

Where it aligns
HIPAA. Breach notice for health data outside HIPAA.
Where it diverges
GDPR. Articles 32 and 33 require security and notice; this rule requires notice only.
Source: 16 CFR Part 318 (eCFR), §§318.1–318.7; FTC, Health Breach Notification Rule, final rule, Federal Register, May 30, 2024 (FR Doc. 2024-10855).
Open the full statute page
MDR Annex I
Binding law. In force.
In plain language

Medical devices sold in the EU must meet general safety and performance requirements. For devices with software, those requirements include information security.

Where it aligns
FD&C Act §524B. Both sides bind classified devices.
Where it diverges
Cyber Resilience Act. That law may reach products the MDR does not; it excludes products the MDR covers.
Source: Regulation (EU) 2017/745, Article 2(1) and Annex I, sections 17.2 and 17.4.
Open the full statute page
Cyber Resilience Act
Binding law. Main duties Dec 2027.
In plain language

Makers of connected products sold in the EU must build in security, handle vulnerabilities and report actively exploited ones. Whether that includes a wearable that a sponsor hands to trial participants is not settled.

Where it aligns
MDR Annex I. Products the MDR covers are excluded, so the two do not overlap.
Where it diverges
FD&C Act §524B. The U.S. rule applies once a device is submitted to FDA. The Act applies to products made available on the market.
Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, Articles 2, 3(22), 14 and 71; Annex I; recitals 13 and 25.
Open the full statute page
GDPR (Arts. 32–33, Ch. V)
Binding law. In force.
In plain language

GDPR protects the personal data of people in the EU. It requires appropriate security for that data, and it sets the conditions for sending personal data outside the EU.

Where it aligns
HIPAA. Both require security safeguards for health data.
Where it diverges
HIPAA. GDPR reaches any data controller, including those that are not covered entities.
Source: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 32, 33 and 44 to 49.
Open the full statute page
European Health Data Space
Binding law. Mar 2027; reuse 2029.
In plain language

The EHDS sets EU-wide rules for using health data in care and for reuse in research and policy. For research reuse, data are made available through a secure processing environment under a data permit.

Where it aligns
GDPR. Both protect health data.
Where it diverges
U.S. instruments. No U.S. counterpart in this set. The EHDS secures the pool of data. Its security rules do not reach the wearable that feeds it (author's reading).
Source: Regulation (EU) 2025/327, Articles 47, 51, 73, 88 to 91 and 105.
Open the full statute page
IMDRF N60, N70, N73
Guidance. Final 2020, 2023.
In plain language

IMDRF, the International Medical Device Regulators Forum, is a voluntary group of medical device regulators from around the world. It publishes shared principles. Three documents cover device cybersecurity: general principles (N60), legacy devices (N70) and software bills of materials (N73).

Where it aligns
FDA and the EU both sit on IMDRF's Management Committee.
Where it diverges
Trial technology. It was written for devices only, and N60 leaves out data-privacy harm.
Source: IMDRF/CYBER WG/N60FINAL:2020, Principles and Practices for Medical Device Cybersecurity (18 March 2020), sections 2.0 and 4.0.
Open the full statute page
ICH E6(R3)
Guideline. Adopted Jan 2025.
In plain language

ICH E6(R3) is the international good clinical practice guideline. It sets out how trials should be run to protect participants and produce reliable data. It has a section on computerised systems that includes security.

Where it aligns
Part 11. Both expect trustworthy electronic data.
Where it diverges
eConsent and eCOA platforms. It names no technical security standard for them.
Source: ICH, Guideline for Good Clinical Practice E6(R3), final version adopted 6 January 2025, section 4.3.3 and glossary (source records).

When the rules take effect

Binding rules aimed at connected products and health data arrived after decentralized trials took off. Filled dots have happened. Open dots are still ahead.

  1. Mar 2020 IMDRF N60

    IMDRF publishes its general principles for medical device cybersecurity (N60, March 18, 2020).

  2. Mar 2023 §524B applies

    Premarket submissions for cyber devices must include cybersecurity information (effective March 29, 2023). IMDRF N70 and N73 are also final in 2023.

  3. Jul 2024 FTC breach rule amended

    The FTC's amendments to its Health Breach Notification Rule, making clear that it covers health apps and similar technologies, take effect July 29, 2024.

  4. Sept to Oct 2024 FDA guidances

    FDA issues its decentralized trials guidance (September) and its electronic systems Q&A (October). Both are nonbinding.

  5. Jan 2025 ICH E6(R3)

    The revised good clinical practice guideline is adopted on January 6, 2025. Section 4.3.3 covers security.

  6. Dec 2025 MDR amendment proposed

    The Commission proposes amending the MDR and IVDR, including new cybersecurity reporting (COM(2025) 1023). As of August 2026 it had not been adopted.

  7. Sept 2026 CRA reporting

    Cyber Resilience Act duties to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents apply from September 11, 2026.

  8. Mar 2027 EHDS applies

    The European Health Data Space Regulation applies from March 26, 2027.

  9. Dec 2027 CRA main duties

    The Cyber Resilience Act applies in full from December 11, 2027. Its reach to a tool supplied only inside a trial is unsettled.

  10. Mar 2029 EHDS research reuse

    Chapter IV on secondary use applies from March 26, 2029, with the secure processing environment (Art. 73). Wearable and wellness data categories are included.

  11. Mar 2031 EHDS trial data

    Data from clinical trials becomes a category for secondary use from March 26, 2031 (Art. 51(1)(m) and Art. 105).

Three policy gaps

Policy gap 1: the sensor
No binding U.S. rule makes the maker of an unsubmitted trial wearable find, disclose or patch its flaws. Policy Recommendation 1
Why it is a policy gapNeither §524B nor the MDR reaches a wearable that was never classified as a device. The Cyber Resilience Act might, but that is unsettled. The FTC's breach rule, where it applies, requires notice after a breach and says nothing about finding or patching flaws.
Policy gap 2: the systems
None of the instruments reviewed names a technical security standard for eConsent and eCOA platforms. Standards such as IEC 81001-5-1 and ISO/IEC 27001 exist, but no U.S. or EU trial rule or guidance points to one. Policy Recommendation 1
Why it is a policy gapPart 11 sets general controls, GDPR asks for appropriate security, and ICH E6(R3) lists controls to consider. None names a standard a platform vendor should meet.
Policy gap 3: the inspections
FDA's GCP collaboration shares inspection findings with EU regulators. Cybersecurity findings are not part of what it describes. Policy Recommendation 2
Why it is a policy gapFDA, EMA, PMDA, MHRA and Health Canada already share inspection information under confidentiality arrangements. The FDA program page cites data integrity and does not mention cybersecurity.
