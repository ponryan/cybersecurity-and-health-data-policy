Counterarguments and responses

These are the objections I think are strongest. Each gets a response grounded in a source and a statement of what the response does not settle.

“Voluntary guidance will not change what wearable makers do.”

Nobody is legally liable for ignoring IMDRF guidance. Three things give it force anyway.

Sponsors buy the technology, and they can write the baseline into contracts. FDA’s Q&A already recommends written agreements with IT providers that cover data security. The Cyber Resilience Act’s recital 13 confirms any entity can set requirements beyond the Act for what it procures. A sponsor can name a general standard today, but no trial-specific baseline exists that regulators on both sides recognize. Policy Recommendation 1 would provide one.

Published patterns tell sponsors what to ask for. Under Policy Recommendation 2, regulators keep vendor-specific findings between themselves and publish only recurring weaknesses, without naming a trial, a sponsor or a vendor. Those patterns show sponsors which weaknesses to write into their contracts.

GDPR already has a route for it. Article 32(3) lets an approved code of conduct or certification serve as an element of showing compliance. A trial-technology baseline could be the basis for one.

Limits of this response. Only binding rules would compel a vendor that sells to buyers who never ask. I treat voluntary guidance as a first step. If the EU settles that the Cyber Resilience Act reaches trial tools, that would add a binding rule on one side.
IMDRF Cyber Resilience Act GDPR

“FDA already covers this.”

FDA’s October 2024 Q&A is detailed. It recommends a risk assessment for all electronic systems, access controls, written agreements with IT providers, and says its digital health technology recommendations apply whether the sponsor provides the tool or participants use their own.

It is guidance. The companion decentralized-trials guidance says that, “in general, FDA’s guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities.” It speaks to the regulated entity’s systems and contracts. In the parts reviewed it does not set a vulnerability process for a sensor maker, and it is U.S. only.

Recommendation 1 would make FDA’s advice more specific and give the EU the same reference. It does not replace it. FDA could also act on its own, for example by pointing to a baseline once one exists.

FDA guidance Part 11

“Security standards already exist, and sponsors can require them now.”

Partly true. ISO/IEC 27001 sets requirements for an organization’s information security management system, and a SOC 2 report gives an auditor’s opinion on a service organization’s controls for security and related criteria. IEC 81001-5-1 (2021) sets security activities across the life cycle of health software, including disclosing security-related issues, receiving notifications about vulnerabilities and delivering security updates. Its definition of health software reaches products that are not medical devices. A sponsor can write any of these into a vendor contract today.

Two things are still missing. Neither FDA’s trial guidance nor ICH E6(R3) points to any of them, so each sponsor picks its own and vendors answer a different questionnaire for each. And regulators in the U.S. and the EU have no harmonized reference to inspect against. Recommendation 1 should select and reference these standards, and write new text only for duties specific to trials.

Limits of this response. All three standards are voluntary, and I do not know how many trial-technology vendors already meet them.
FDA guidance ICH E6(R3) IMDRF

“The EU is simplifying its rules, and the Cyber Resilience Act and MDR changes will close this.”

In December 2025 the Commission proposed amending the MDR and IVDR (COM(2025) 1023), adding cybersecurity reporting for devices and naming cybersecurity in Annex I. It calls the current reporting situation “an important cybersecurity gap.”

The proposal applies to devices under the MDR and IVDR. As of August 2026 it had not been adopted. The Cyber Resilience Act excludes products the MDR covers, and its text never mentions clinical trials, so its reach to a tool supplied only inside a trial is unsettled.

My policy recommendations are voluntary, add no EU law, and work whichever way the Act question is answered.

Limits of this response. If the EU clarifies that the Act reaches trial tools, Recommendation 1 would support compliance, and a shared reading of what good looks like would still help.
MDR Cyber Resilience Act

“Regulators will not share findings across borders.”

EMA and FDA began sharing GCP inspection information in 2009. PMDA asked to join in September 2016 and was admitted as an observer in April 2017. The collaboration runs under confidentiality arrangements among the European Commission, EMA, FDA and PMDA, and the regulators exchange inspection summaries and reports.

Recommendation 2 adds a category of finding to what is already exchanged. It does not add a power, change an arrangement, or move an inspection.

Limits of this response. Sharing details of a vendor’s weaknesses is sensitive. That needs handling rules, and only aggregate patterns should be published.
ICH E6(R3)

“Small and academic sponsors cannot afford it.”

The policy recommendations put the baseline on the tools and their makers. A sponsor would ask a vendor to show how it meets a shared baseline, instead of designing its own assessment.

The baseline would be written in tiers. ICH E6(R3) itself says the approach to validating computerised systems should be based on a risk assessment, so proportionality is already a principle of good clinical practice.

It is voluntary, so no sponsor is forced to adopt it. But sponsor demand is the mechanism that gives the baseline force (see the first objection), so some cost will reach sponsors through vendor pricing.

Limits of this response. Vendors may pass costs on to sponsors. Tiers and a public comment period are the way to test that. I have not modeled costs.
ICH E6(R3)

“Why act now, when no trial wearable has been breached?”

I know of no documented breach of a trial wearable, so my case is one of prevention.

Trial data has been exposed through a site network’s database, a sponsor’s own systems and a vendor’s cloud. Consumer wearable platforms have already been hit: Garmin’s 2020 ransomware outage and the 2018 Polar Flow exposure. Decentralized trials add devices and vendors, and the technology is growing at roughly 14 percent a year in the market report I used. IMDRF’s cybersecurity series took from 2020 to 2023, and the EMA-FDA collaboration began in 2009, so standards take years.

Acting before a large breach allows the baseline to be written without time pressure.

Limits of this response. I found no study counting how many trial tools sit outside device regulation, and no documented breach of a wearable used in a trial. The argument rests on exposure.
Policy problem

“Good clinical practice already covers security, so Policy Recommendation 2 adds nothing.”

ICH E6(R3) §4.3.3 does ask for security controls on computerised systems. Recommendation 2 therefore needs no new standard and uses §4.3.3 as the yardstick.

FDA’s inspectors already look at security. Its compliance program for inspecting sponsors and CROs (CP 7348.810, 2021) tells investigators to check for external security controls such as firewalls, encryption and antivirus software, to review access controls and multifactor authentication, to ask about security patches, and to document any attempted or confirmed unauthorized access.

Recommendation 2 adds sharing. A finding made in one jurisdiction is currently not described as part of what regulators exchange, and without a common definition two regulators cannot compare what they find. The FDA program does not use the word ‘cybersecurity,’ so the pilot’s first task is to name these checks as one category and align them with what EU inspectors review.
